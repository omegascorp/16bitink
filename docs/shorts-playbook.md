## What works so far

1. **A hook the viewer already knows wins the first second.** "There's always a bigger fish" is a known meme, so it had the best stayed-to-watch (48%) and the most engaged views. Stayed-to-watch is the number that decides whether YouTube shows a short to more people.
2. **A build-up with one sudden payoff drives rewatches.** "Wait for the shark…" lost more people at the start but was watched 1.25 times on average, the best of the three. The seamless loop matters here.
3. **Short beats long.** Both 13s shorts beat the 25s one on stayed-to-watch and % viewed.
4. **Explainers lose the swipe.** "How deep…" opened with a question and a slow descent: two-thirds of viewers swiped away, and it didn't loop (85% viewed). The people who stayed liked it (best likes per engaged view), so the idea is fine; the opening isn't.
5. **"This really happened" plus a role reversal is our strongest format.** A real event where the predator becomes prey, recreated in the game's ink style. It's the format behind "bigger fish".
6. **A calm opening loses the swipe, even when the payoff is strong** (early, needs confirming). "Fish that eat birds" opened on an empty sea, the tern half off-screen and documentary captions ("Seychelles. A young tern skims the sea…"). The threat didn't appear until ~2s and the payoff at 4.6s. Only 16% stayed, half the other shorts' rate. Viewers who stayed left after ~9s, before the loop came round. Compare "bigger fish", which opens with the shark already on the hook.
7. **Narrated plus a reference everyone knows is our best format so far** (export of 2026-10-14). "Nemo's dad should have become his mom" got 55.6% stayed to watch, beating every story short, and held 98.7% of a 20s video. "White sand beaches are fish poop" (a beach everyone has stood on) started at 52%. The deep-sea narrated "anglerfish mates" got 41% and lost viewers before the end (81% viewed of 22s). Write hooks around something the viewer already has in their head (a film, a pet, the beach, money, a forest), not around a strange animal they've never heard of.
8. **Our stayed-to-watch is far below what big Shorts channels aim for.** They treat 81.1% as the bar for a video that goes viral (Deven Seenath, see below); our best is 55.6%. The first second is still where we lose most viewers.

## Advice from Shorts creators (added 2026-10-15)

Two YouTube videos the user shared. The creators make money from Shorts coaching, so treat their claims as advice to test, not facts. What we took from each:

**Deven Seenath, "How I Make People Watch My Shorts (3B Views/Month)": keeping viewers past the first second.** His "swipe ratio" is Studio's *stayed to watch*. He aims for **81.1% or higher**.

1. **Picture the hook before making the video.** If the idea is good, you can see the first frame in your head. If you can't, the idea is too weak. For us, frame 1 shows exactly what the first line says, so the hook works even with the sound off ("a test your dog fails": the fish at its mirror *and* a dog).
2. **The picture matches the words, word for word.** When the narrator names something (a person, a place, a number, "a cloud of tiny fish"), that exact thing is on screen at that moment. A hook about "this guy" over a shot of a girl, or about a baby with no baby in the shot, loses the viewer. He sees this mistake in most videos he reviews.
3. **Drop worn-out hooks.** "You won't believe what happened when…", "When bro…" and "Isn't it crazy when…" no longer stop the scroll. A hook works by **breaking the pattern** the viewer expects. **Be specific and descriptive**: add a name, a number or a detail ("this blue-shirt guy" rather than "this guy"). Our version: name the fish, the number, the place, the film.
4. **Inflection control.** A flat delivery loses viewers at every flat stretch, so retention drops in steps. Pitch that rises and falls keeps pulling the viewer back in and keeps the retention curve flat. **How we apply it:** ElevenLabs eleven_v4 takes audio tags (`[curious]`, `[surprised]`, `[dramatically]`, `[softly]`, `[mischievously]`…) and CAPS on the key word. Change the tag where the mood changes, roughly once per sentence. Example: *"[curious] This fish passed a test your DOG fails. [softly] Scientists put a brown mark…"*. First used on queue #23–29.
5. **Sound effects match the emotion of the beat.** Use a tense sound for a threat, a bright "ding" for a surprising number and a soft one for a tender moment. Never put a funny sound on a grim beat. To judge a topic's mood, read the comments on popular videos about it.
6. **No commentary means constant motion.** A short without a voice has to be visually busy all the way through.

**A creator running six monetised faceless Shorts channels: how to upload.**

- **Content first.** If every video flops, the problem is the video, not the upload settings.
- **Title:** capitalise the first letter of every word and end with one fitting emoji, e.g. *"This Fish Passed A Test Your Dog Fails 🐶"*. **Never give away the payoff in the title.** He says hashtags in titles do nothing, so from #23 we leave `#shorts` out of the title.
- **Description, tags, hashtags and category hardly matter for Shorts.** Nearly all views come from the Shorts feed, not search. Our search-style descriptions take two minutes and don't hurt, so keep them, but don't spend more time on them.
- **"Made for kids": always No. Age restriction: always No.**
- **Altered or synthetic content:** say Yes when the video shows realistic AI-made footage or a real person made to say things. Our ink animation isn't realistic and the AI narrator isn't a real person, so we answer No. Revisit this if we ever use realistic footage.
- **Related video:** link another of our shorts on a similar topic. A few people tap it.
- **Don't schedule Shorts.** He and his students get a weaker push from scheduled posts, sometimes zero views. Upload as **Unlisted**, then switch to **Public** at the posting time.
- **Posting time barely matters.** It changes how fast the views come, not how many.
- **If a channel gets stuck at zero views, try uploading from a different device.** He uploads from the YouTube Studio app, which keeps 60 fps, then sets the thumbnail in the YouTube app. Our shorts are 30 fps, so the app's 30 fps cap doesn't affect us.
- **Audience comes from the content, not from hashtags or posting time.** English narration and Western references (films, places, money) bring a higher-RPM US audience; `#USA` doesn't.

## Rules for the next short

- **10–13 seconds for story shorts without a voice, 18–20s for narrated ones** (the 20s clownfish held 98.7%; the 22s and 25s shorts dropped to 81–86%). Either way, one seamless loop: the last frame flows into the first (see the loop tests in `games/inkfish/test/promo*.test.ts`).
- **Action from the first frame.** No question or explainer before something moves. The threat should already be on screen in frame 1. Since every short loops, choose the file's start point (`start` in `record.mjs`) to land just before the payoff, not at the story's beginning.
- **The opening caption is a hook, not a setting.** "Seychelles. A young tern skims the sea…" reads like a documentary. Save the place name for after the payoff.
- **One build-up, one payoff.** A threat appears, the prey reacts ("!"), then a single big moment around 40% of the way in.
- **A real event when possible**, with *"This really happened."* after the payoff. Fact-check it before posting.
- **A meme-style line at the end** that also works as the title, e.g. "there's always a bigger fish", "some fish eat birds".
- **On-screen text and the game's own sound effects** for story shorts. **Narrated educational shorts** (since 2026-10-13) now do better than the story shorts ("What works" #7). A voiceover makes the loop seam obvious, so end the script mid-sentence and let the opening line finish it.
- **Text stays between y ≈ 270 and 1250** of the 1080×1920 frame. Shorts and Reels cover the top ~14% and bottom ~35% with buttons.
- **Show something you actually do in the game** (eat, grow, leap for birds, dodge hooks), so the short sells the game, not just a fact.
- **Title:** the hook in Title Case plus one emoji, no hashtags and no spoilers, e.g. *"Sharks Are Older Than Trees 🌳"*. Up to #22 the titles ended in `#shorts`; #23 onwards don't.
- **The hook is a reference the viewer already knows** (a film, a pet, the beach, money), it's specific (a name, a number), and frame 1 shows it literally. See "Advice from Shorts creators".
- **Narration is directed, not flat:** audio tags and CAPS emphasis in the ElevenLabs script, changing mood at least once per sentence.
- **Every noun the narrator says is on screen as it's said.** Sound effects match the mood of each beat.
- **Upload:** Unlisted first, then switch to Public at the posting time (don't schedule). Made for kids: No. Altered content: No. Link a related short.
- **Descriptions are for search, not for reading.** Line 1 is the phrase people type ("blobfish underwater", "greenland shark age"), line 2 is search variants, then 3 hashtags (YouTube shows them above the title). Don't mention the game or the site in descriptions or tags; it's all about the content's discoverability. Nobody reads them. Tags work the same way: the most-searched phrase first, then question searches and misspellings ("blob fish", "oar fish"). Examples are in `games/inkfish/promo/schedule/titles.md`.
- **No links in the description.** One short with a link sat at ~20 views until the link was removed. Name the site ("16bit.ink") without a URL.
- **Branding on screen: small, and never in the hook.** Anything that looks like an ad in the first second costs swipes. Gameplay shorts show "InkFish · 16bit.ink" in every frame, since the game is the content. From #23, narrated shorts show the same credit under the captions, fading in 40% of the way through and gone before the loop point (`dev/promo/credit.ts`, applied in `main.ts`). Few viewers will type a URL. The pinned comment ("Play it free at 16bit.ink", as plain text), the channel banner and About links, and a "Related video" pointing at the gameplay video do more. Check whether it works through youtube.com referrers and direct visits in the site's admin analytics.

## Collecting the data

Export from YouTube Studio once each video is ~72 hours old, and again at 7 days:

1. **Analytics → Advanced mode**, content type **Shorts**, period **Lifetime**, grouped by **Content**. Include: Shown in feed, Stayed to watch %, Views, Engaged views, Average percentage viewed, Average view duration, Likes, Comments, Shares, Subscribers gained. **Export → CSV.**
2. **Traffic source** for the same period, to check views come from the Shorts feed.
3. **Retention curve** screenshot for each short (per-video analytics → Engagement), to see the second people swipe at.
4. **Site visits:** since the descriptions carry no links (see the rules above), watch 16bit.ink traffic with a youtube.com referrer in the days after each post.

Map each YouTube title to its file name in `games/inkfish/promo/`, so results tie back to the choices that made them.

## Making a short

The pipeline is in `games/inkfish/dev/promo/`. Each short has its own folder (`bigger/`, `depth/`, `birds/`):

- `story.ts`: every beat as a pure function of time;
- `render.ts`: draws a frame;
- `audio.ts`: the soundtrack, mixed from the game's sound effects.

To add one, register it in `main.ts` (as `?short=<name>`) and in `record.mjs`'s variant list. Preview at `/promo.html?short=<name>` (`&t=4.2` freezes a moment) and record with `pnpm --filter @16bitink/inkfish promo -- --only <name>`. Record when nothing else is editing files under `dev/`: Vite hot-reloads the page on any edit, and a reload mid-recording fails with "Execution context was destroyed". Loop length must be a whole number of video frames (30 fps) and of line-boil cycles (0.375s), which means a **multiple of 1.5s** (12s, 18s, 21s, 22.5s…).

**Narrated shorts** (`mates/` is the example):

1. Write the script so it ends mid-sentence and the first line finishes it. Fact-check every claim first.
2. Generate it with the ElevenLabs connector: one flow, four takes, voice *Cedric M – Engaging Documentary Narrator* (`VvVVKaa2nHZpbZdtSRSX`), model eleven_v4. **Direct the delivery**: put an audio tag where the mood changes (`[curious]`, `[surprised]`, `[dramatically]`, `[softly]`, `[nervous]`, `[mischievously]`, `[warmly]`) and CAPS on the word to stress (see "Advice from Shorts creators"). Tags aren't spoken. Pick the tightest take by its pauses (`ffmpeg … silencedetect`).
3. Speed it up a little with `ffmpeg -af atempo=…` (pitch is kept). Choose the tempo so the voice ends about 0.3s before the loop point. Pick the loop first (a multiple of 1.5s, see above), then the tempo: `tempo = last word's end ÷ (loop − 0.3)`. About 1.07–1.13 sounds natural.
4. Transcribe the take with ElevenLabs Scribe for word timings, and keep them in `words.ts`. Every beat and the captions are timed from the words.
5. In `audio.ts`, the voice goes straight to the output; game sound effects stay quiet under it.
