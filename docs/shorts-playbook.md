## What works so far

1. **A hook the viewer already knows wins the first second.** "There's always a bigger fish" is a known meme, so it had the best stayed-to-watch (48%) and the most engaged views. Stayed-to-watch is the number that decides whether YouTube shows a short to more people.
2. **A build-up with one sudden payoff drives rewatches.** "Wait for the shark…" lost more people at the start but was watched 1.25 times on average, the best of the three. The seamless loop matters here.
3. **Short beats long.** Both 13s shorts beat the 25s one on stayed-to-watch and % viewed.
4. **Explainers lose the swipe.** "How deep…" opened with a question and a slow descent: two-thirds of viewers swiped away, and it didn't loop (85% viewed). The people who stayed liked it (best likes per engaged view), so the idea is fine; the opening isn't.
5. **"This really happened" plus a role reversal is our strongest format.** A real event where the predator becomes prey, recreated in the game's ink style. It's the format behind "bigger fish".
6. **A calm opening loses the swipe, even when the payoff is strong** (early, needs confirming). "Fish that eat birds" opened on an empty sea, the tern half off-screen and documentary captions ("Seychelles. A young tern skims the sea…"). The threat didn't appear until ~2s and the payoff at 4.6s. Only 16% stayed, half the other shorts' rate. Viewers who stayed left after ~9s, before the loop came round. Compare "bigger fish", which opens with the shark already on the hook.
7. **Narrated plus a reference everyone knows is our best format so far** (export of 2026-10-14). "Nemo's dad should have become his mom" got 55.6% stayed to watch, beating every story short, and held 98.7% of a 20s video. "White sand beaches are fish poop" (a beach everyone has stood on) started at 52%. The deep-sea narrated "anglerfish mates" got 41% and lost viewers before the end (81% viewed of 22s). Write hooks around something the viewer already has in their head (a film, a pet, the beach, money, a forest), not around a strange animal they've never heard of.
8. **On our channel, about 70% stayed to watch is where a short breaks out** (export of 2026-10-18). The two shorts at 70.5–71.7% got 14k and 32k views. Every short below 65% stopped at 1.3–4k. Big Shorts channels aim for 81.1% (Deven Seenath, see below), but 70% is enough to leave YouTube's test batch here. Aim for 70%+ on every short.
9. **"What you think you know is wrong" is good, not great** (exports of 2026-10-16 and 10-18). "White sand beaches are fish poop" (64.6%) and "This is NOT what a blobfish looks like" (60.9%) beat every other narrated short until 10-16, and blobfish still has half the channel's shares: people send a myth-buster to a friend. But they topped out at 2–3k views. The 10-16 note that "deep-sea animals nobody has heard of stay at the bottom" was wrong: the gulper eel (#13) is a deep-sea animal few people know, and it broke out. An unknown animal works when its strangeness is **visible** in frame 1. It fails when the strangeness has to be explained (faceless cusk 55%, anglerfish mates 43%, barreleye 45.5%).
10. **Narrated shorts over 20s don't get rewatched.** The 17s parrotfish is at 107% viewed. At 20–22s, clownfish, blobfish and anglerfish mates fall to 81–98%, and the early pufferfish and faceless cusk are at 68–77%. Viewers leave before the loop comes round. Aim for 16.5–18s narrated.
11. **We have no returning audience and almost no comments.** Almost every viewer is new (4,761 new against 6 returning, 2026-10-18), and comments fell to 1 per ~10,000 views: the 46k views on the two breakouts brought 2 comments. That's the next gap after stayed to watch (see the "view jail" notes in "Advice from Shorts creators"). The pinned-comment questions start with #37, which isn't posted yet.
12. **Frame 1 is one giant mouth mid-gulp: our best opening** (export of 2026-10-18). Both breakouts open on it: the viperfish's jaws round a fangtooth its own size (#21) and the gulper's ballooned mouth (#13), each alone on dark water. It's the same picture the most-viewed fish-eat-fish thumbnails use (see "Thumbnails" in `docs/longform-playbook.md`). Next best is an action the viewer can see (the parrotfish pooping sand next to "you", 64.6%; the flying fish in the air with a timer, 59.9%).
13. **A frame 1 that looks like a chart gets swiped.** Labels, numbered parts, scorecards, timelines and big numbers over a map make a short look like a lesson: octopus hearts 37.4%, Greenland shark's timeline 40.0%, red crabs' "50,000,000" 44.9%, barreleye's labelled head 45.5%, the wrasse's mirror-test scorecard 52.2%. Put the number or the label in the narration and the second beat, not in the opening frame.
14. **A gameplay short can be the best short.** #21 (13s, game footage, meme captions, no voice) got 31.8k views, +25 subscribers and 138% viewed. It sells the game too: "InkFish · 16bit.ink" is in every frame. It's still one short, so treat "gameplay with a giant swallow" as the format to repeat, not "gameplay" in general, until #16–20 and #22 are out.

## Advice from Shorts creators (added 2026-10-15, third video 2026-10-16, fourth 2026-10-22)

Four YouTube videos the user shared. The creators make money from Shorts coaching, so treat their claims as advice to test, not facts. What we took from each:

**Deven Seenath, "How I Make People Watch My Shorts (3B Views/Month)": keeping viewers past the first second.** His "swipe ratio" is Studio's *stayed to watch*. He aims for **81.1% or higher**.

1. **Picture the hook before making the video.** If the idea is good, you can see the first frame in your head. If you can't, the idea is too weak. For us, frame 1 shows exactly what the first line says, so the hook works even with the sound off ("a test your dog fails": the fish at its mirror *and* a dog).
2. **The picture matches the words, word for word.** When the narrator names something (a person, a place, a number, "a cloud of tiny fish"), that exact thing is on screen at that moment. A hook about "this guy" over a shot of a girl, or about a baby with no baby in the shot, loses the viewer. He sees this mistake in most videos he reviews.
3. **Drop worn-out hooks.** "You won't believe what happened when…", "When bro…" and "Isn't it crazy when…" no longer stop the scroll. A hook works by **breaking the pattern** the viewer expects. **Be specific and descriptive**: add a name, a number or a detail ("this blue-shirt guy" rather than "this guy"). Our version: name the fish, the number, the place, the film.
4. **Inflection control.** A flat delivery loses viewers at every flat stretch, so retention drops in steps. Pitch that rises and falls keeps pulling the viewer back in and keeps the retention curve flat. **How we apply it:** ElevenLabs eleven_v4 takes audio tags (`[curious]`, `[surprised]`, `[dramatically]`, `[softly]`, `[mischievously]`…) and CAPS on the key word. Change the tag where the mood changes, roughly once per sentence, and mix high-energy tags with quiet ones (see "Directing the voice" in "Making a short"). Example: *"[curious] This fish passed a test your DOG fails. [softly] Scientists put a brown mark…"*. First used on queue #23–29.
5. **Sound effects match the emotion of the beat.** Use a tense sound for a threat, a bright "ding" for a surprising number and a soft one for a tender moment. Never put a funny sound on a grim beat. To judge a topic's mood, read the comments on popular videos about it.
6. **No commentary means constant motion.** A short without a voice has to be visually busy all the way through.

**A creator running six monetised faceless Shorts channels: how to upload.**

- **Content first.** If every video flops, the problem is the video, not the upload settings.
- **Title:** capitalise the first letter of every word and end with one fitting emoji, e.g. *"This Fish Passed A Test Your Dog Fails 🐶"*. **Never give away the payoff in the title.** He says hashtags in titles do nothing, so from #23 we leave `#shorts` out of the title.
- **Description, tags, hashtags and category hardly matter for Shorts.** Nearly all views come from the Shorts feed, not search. Our search-style descriptions take two minutes and don't hurt, so keep them, but don't spend more time on them.
- **"Made for kids": always No. Age restriction: always No.**
- **Altered or synthetic content:** say Yes when the video shows realistic AI-made footage or a real person made to say things. Our ink animation isn't realistic and the AI narrator isn't a real person, so we answer No. Revisit this if we ever use realistic footage.
- **Related video:** link another of our shorts on a similar topic. A few people tap it.
- **Don't schedule Shorts.** He and his students get a weaker push from scheduled posts, sometimes zero views. Upload as **Unlisted**, then switch to **Public** at the posting time.
- **Posting time barely matters.** It changes how fast the views come, not how many.
- **If a channel gets stuck at zero views, try uploading from a different device.** He uploads from the YouTube Studio app, which keeps 60 fps, then sets the thumbnail in the YouTube app. Our shorts are 30 fps, so the app's 30 fps cap doesn't affect us.
- **Audience comes from the content, not from hashtags or posting time.** English narration and Western references (films, places, money) bring a higher-RPM US audience; `#USA` doesn't.

**Deven Seenath, "How to break out of every view jail on YT Shorts": why a channel stalls at one view count.** A "view jail" is when most shorts stop at the same number of views. He judges it over **8–12 days of uploads**. One short that stalls doesn't mean the channel has. "Trust score", "test and rebalance" and "views per viewer" are his own terms, not YouTube metrics. His levels and fixes:

| Stuck at | What he blames | His fix |
|---|---|---|
| 0 views | Low "trust score" | Basics he covers elsewhere, not in this video |
| ~30k | Some key numbers are good, but not all | Stayed to watch **≥ 81.1%**. Average view duration longer than the video (avg % viewed **above 100%**), at a length of 24–32s. Monthly audience growing. About **half new viewers, half casual + regular** (Studio → Audience) |
| ~50k | Too few comments | About **1 comment per 1,500 views**. Not "comment below" calls to action; subtle prompts and an **emotional journey**. A comment is a reaction, and people only react when something changes, so the mood must change through the video (happy → angry → sad → shock → tension). A video that stays on one mood ("funny, funny, funny") gets swiped and gets no comments |
| ~100k | Too few uploads | Up to **5 shorts a day, 2–3 hours apart** (2.5–3h is best). Not 45 minutes apart: he says YouTube recently started testing each short for longer, which shows as a "staircase" in the first 24 hours of views |
| ~1M | Monthly audience too small | Use every kind of post except live streams, above all **community posts**. **Image polls** work best, with questions anyone can answer whatever the channel's topic (left hand vs right hand, red vs blue, pineapple on pizza) |
| ~10M | Each viewer watches too few of your videos | Link similar shorts as Related videos. Make broad, rewatchable videos that anyone, of any age and in any language, can follow **without words or sound** |

**Where we are:** about 1,200–1,500 views per short, so we're below his first level, and the 30k checklist is ours too. The big gap is stayed to watch (27–56% against his 81.1%). Avg % viewed is already above 100% on several shorts, because they loop. Monthly audience, the new/returning split and comments per view haven't been exported yet.

**How we use it:**

1. **Judge a format over 8–12 days of posts, not by one short.** One stalled short doesn't kill a format.
2. **Plan the emotional journey before writing the script: an emotional rollercoaster** (the user's direction, 2026-10-18). Each short moves through at least three moods, one per beat. From #49 aim for a new mood every sentence, about every 3–4 seconds, and swing between opposites (thrill → fear → laugh, eerie → tender → cheeky), not between neighbours (curious → interested). Example: curious hook → disgust or a laugh at the fact → a tender or sad turn → a shocking last number. This plan also drives the ElevenLabs audio tags (the tag changes where the mood changes), the sound effects (they match each mood) and the picture (each mood looks different: colour, camera, the character's face). Write the moods next to each line of the script.
3. **Ask for comments subtly, never "comment below".** End on a claim people want to argue with, or put a light question in the pinned comment ("Would you eat one?", "Which is worse, the male or the female?"). Keep the site line in the pinned comment as well.
   - **Rage bait, as a test (first in #50, the user's request on 2026-10-18).** One beat states a cheeky, confident opinion that viewers will want to correct: *"Honestly, Earth's plants are doing it WRONG."* People comment to argue, and the comment count feeds the algorithm. Guardrails: the opinion must be arguable, never a false fact (the facts in the short stay checked). It provokes about the topic, never about a group of people. No "fight me" or other comment calls to action. The pinned comment picks a side and asks the viewer theirs (*"Team green 🌿 or team black 🖤?"*). Compare #50's comments per view with #49 and #51, built the same week without bait. If it clearly wins, put one arguable line in every few shorts (e.g. "the male anglerfish has the worst life in the ocean"), not in all of them.
4. **Track comments per view** (target 1 per 1,500) alongside stayed to watch.
5. **If we post more than one short in a day, space them 2–3 hours apart** and post no more than 5 a day. The user picks the posting days ([see the schedule](../promo/schedule/titles.md)). At 5 a day the 36-short queue would be gone in a week, so more posts a day is a trade-off, not a rule.
6. **Post community image polls** with our ink art, using broad questions anyone answers ("Shark or octopus?", "Beach or deep sea?", "Would you swim with this fish?"). They grow the monthly audience between shorts. No links in them either.
7. **Make gameplay shorts readable with the sound off and without reading.** The picture alone should tell the story, so they work in any language.

**Where his advice disagrees with our data:** he wants 24–32s shorts. Our 13s and 20s shorts held best, and the 22s and 25s ones dropped to 81–86% viewed. Keep our lengths until a longer short holds above 100% viewed. A 24s narrated short is worth testing once: #51 (heavy planet) is 24s, and #49–50 are 21s and 22.5s.

**A Shorts coach with four years of channels, "the advice that took me four years to learn" (added 2026-10-22): what to make, and how fast.** The second half of the video is a pitch for his mentorship program; we skipped it.

1. **What you make matters more than how well you make it.** Good stats help you lock down a format, but they can't save a topic nobody wants. A topic with high demand and little competition breaks out even with average stats. In a crowded niche you need much better stats to get the same views.
2. **Copy a young channel, not an old one.** Look for a channel that broke through recently, with only **10–30 uploads** and repeated viral videos. It proves there's demand now. Take its format and add our own twist; don't copy a big old channel one to one.
3. **Find a format you can repeat, not one viral video.** Since about 2024, one viral short no longer lifts the rest of the channel. The top channels repeat their winning format instead of trying something new in every short.
4. **Every winning format wears out.** Keep posting the winner, and keep testing what could replace it, so there's a new winner ready when the old one slows down.
5. **Move faster.** His biggest regret is going slowly once something worked. When a formula works, post more of it and start more channels with it. Pay for help (editors, partners) so the owner works on the higher-value tasks.

**How we use it:**

1. **The giant swallow is our winning format: make it a series.** #13 and #21 are the only breakouts so far, and #44, #45 and #48 repeat the same picture. Name the series ("How Did That Fit? part 3, 4, …") and keep making it, as gameplay and as narrated shorts. Until the data says otherwise, about **two of every three posts repeat the winner**, and one tests something new (a topic, a game, a hook).
2. **Post the finished shorts.** At the 2026-10-18 export 38 finished shorts weren't public yet (#1, 7, 8, 16–20, 22, 24–32, 35–54). They teach nothing and earn nothing until they're posted. At 3–5 a day, 2–3 hours apart, the backlog goes up in about 10 days, which is also the 8–12 days needed to judge a format. Post the swallow and action shorts first (see "Suggested posting order").
3. **Check demand before making a batch.** Most of the idea bank comes from big old channels (Odd Danny's 4–29M-view shorts). Before the next batch, look for **young channels (10–30 uploads) with repeated viral shorts** in nearby niches: animated sea creatures, fish-eat-fish and "size 1 to max" games, animated alien life and space facts. Note each channel's format and add it to the idea bank with a "young channel" tag.
4. **Prefer topics with less competition.** Narrated "weird animal facts" is a crowded niche. Ink-style game footage of a fish swallowing one its own size is rare. That rarity may explain why #21, a 13s gameplay short, got the most views and subscribers. Gameplay also sells the game, so it's the format to grow.
5. **Review results per format, not per short.** At each export, group the shorts by format (swallow, myth-buster, known reference, gameplay, InkCrab, InkMine) and compare their median views. When the winner's views fall for two exports in a row, move the best test format into its place.
6. **More channels only after a format repeats.** We have one channel and one format that has broken out twice. If the InkMine test (#49–51) shows space topics don't travel on the ocean channel but a space format does well, give InkMine its own channel. Don't split the channel before then.
7. **Our "hired help" is automation.** Making a short is already fast. Uploading, pinning comments and exporting stats are still done by hand. Exports could come from the YouTube Analytics API. Uploads through the YouTube Data API stay private until Google audits the API project, so keep uploading by hand unless we go through that audit.

**How it fits our data:** the stats a short needs to break out depend on its niche. A crowded niche needs higher stats, and a niche with high demand and little competition needs lower ones. Our ~70% stayed to watch (rule 8 in "What works so far") only got our two best shorts out of the test batch, to 14k and 32k views. Deven Seenath's 81.1% is his fix for channels stuck at ~30k, where our best shorts stopped, and it's what he aims for to reach millions of views. So the two numbers may both hold, for different view levels: about 70% to leave the test batch here, and maybe 81%+ to go past ~30k. The line can also differ between our own formats: crowded narrated animal facts may need more than rarer ink gameplay. Two breakouts aren't enough to tell. Keep experimenting, record the stayed to watch and views of every short, and at each export check which line each format needs for each view level.

## Rules for the next short

- **10–13 seconds for story shorts without a voice, 18–20s for narrated ones** (the 20s clownfish held 98.7%; the 22s and 25s shorts dropped to 81–86%). Either way, one seamless loop: the last frame flows into the first (see the loop tests in `games/inkfish/test/promo*.test.ts`).
- **Action from the first frame.** No question or explainer before something moves. The threat should already be on screen in frame 1. Since every short loops, choose the file's start point (`start` in `record.mjs`) to land just before the payoff, not at the story's beginning.
- **The opening caption is a hook, not a setting.** "Seychelles. A young tern skims the sea…" reads like a documentary. Save the place name for after the payoff.
- **One build-up, one payoff.** A threat appears, the prey reacts ("!"), then a single big moment around 40% of the way in.
- **A real event when possible**, with *"This really happened."* after the payoff. Fact-check it before posting.
- **A meme-style line at the end** that also works as the title, e.g. "there's always a bigger fish", "some fish eat birds".
- **On-screen text and the game's own sound effects** for story shorts. **Narrated educational shorts** (since 2026-10-13) now do better than the story shorts ("What works" #7). A voiceover makes the loop seam obvious, so end the script mid-sentence and let the opening line finish it.
- **Text stays between y ≈ 270 and 1250** of the 1080×1920 frame. Shorts and Reels cover the top ~14% and bottom ~35% with buttons.
- **Show something you actually do in the game** (eat, grow, leap for birds, dodge hooks), so the short sells the game, not just a fact.
- **Title:** the hook in Title Case plus one emoji, no hashtags and no spoilers, e.g. *"Sharks Are Older Than Trees 🌳"*. Up to #22 the titles ended in `#shorts`; #23 onwards don't.
- **The hook is a reference the viewer already knows** (a film, a pet, the beach, money), it's specific (a name, a number), and frame 1 shows it literally. See "Advice from Shorts creators".
- **Frame 1 is a picture, not a chart** (from the 2026-10-18 export). Best: one giant mouth mid-gulp, or one big subject doing the thing the title says, alone on a plain (ideally dark) background. No labels, numbered parts, scorecards, timelines or big numbers in the first frame; bring them in on the second beat. Before recording, grab frame 1 (`ffmpeg -ss 0.3 -i <file> -frames:v 1 f1.png`) and ask: does this frame alone prove the title, with the sound off?
- **Target 70%+ stayed to watch.** That's where our shorts break out of the test batch (#13 and #21); 60–65% gets a second push to 2–3k and stops.
- **Narration is directed, not flat:** audio tags and CAPS emphasis in the ElevenLabs script, changing mood at least once per sentence.
- **Every noun the narrator says is on screen as it's said.** Sound effects match the mood of each beat.
- **The mood changes at least three times**, one mood per beat, planned in the script before the voice is generated, and the picture and sound effects change with it. From #49, aim for a new mood every sentence (an emotional rollercoaster). No short stays on one mood the whole way.
- **Invite comments subtly:** a claim people want to argue with, or a light question in the pinned comment. Never "comment below". Rage bait (an arguable, cheeky opinion, never a false fact) is being tested in #50 and #54; see "How we use it" #3.
- **Upload:** Unlisted first, then switch to Public at the posting time (don't schedule). Made for kids: No. Altered content: No. Link a related short. On a day with several posts, space them 2–3 hours apart, and post no more than 5 a day.
- **Between shorts, post community image polls** with the game's art and broad questions anyone can answer.
- **Descriptions are for search, not for reading.** Line 1 is the phrase people type ("blobfish underwater", "greenland shark age"), line 2 is search variants, then 3 hashtags (YouTube shows them above the title). Don't mention the game or the site in descriptions or tags; it's all about the content's discoverability. Nobody reads them. Tags work the same way: the most-searched phrase first, then question searches and misspellings ("blob fish", "oar fish"). Examples are in `promo/schedule/titles.md`.
- **No links in the description.** One short with a link sat at ~20 views until the link was removed. Name the site ("16bit.ink") without a URL.
- **Branding on screen: small, and never in the hook.** Anything that looks like an ad in the first second costs swipes. Gameplay shorts show "InkFish · 16bit.ink" in every frame, since the game is the content. From #23, narrated shorts show the same credit under the captions, fading in 40% of the way through and gone before the loop point (`dev/promo/credit.ts`, applied in `main.ts`). Few viewers will type a URL. The pinned comment (as plain text, using the game's short link: "Play it free at 16bit.ink/fish", "/crab", "/mine"), the channel banner and About links, and a "Related video" pointing at the gameplay video do more. Typed visits from apps carry no referrer, so the short links are how we count them: each shows as its own source ("16bit.ink/fish") in the site's admin analytics, next to youtube.com referrers.

## Making a short

The pipeline is in `games/inkfish/dev/promo/`. Each short has its own folder (`bigger/`, `depth/`, `birds/`):

- `story.ts`: every beat as a pure function of time;
- `render.ts`: draws a frame;
- `audio.ts`: the soundtrack, mixed from the game's sound effects.

To add one, register it in `main.ts` (as `?short=<name>`) and in `record.mjs`'s variant list. Preview at `/promo.html?short=<name>` (`&t=4.2` freezes a moment) and record with `pnpm --filter @16bitink/inkfish promo -- --only <name>`. Record when nothing else is editing files under `dev/`: Vite hot-reloads the page on any edit, and a reload mid-recording fails with "Execution context was destroyed". Loop length must be a whole number of video frames (30 fps) and of line-boil cycles (0.375s), which means a **multiple of 1.5s** (12s, 18s, 21s, 22.5s…).

**Narrated shorts** (`mates/` is the example):

1. Write the script so it ends mid-sentence and the first line finishes it. Fact-check every claim first.
2. Generate it with the ElevenLabs connector: one flow, four takes, voice *Mark – Natural Conversations* (`UgBBYS2sOqTuMpoF3BR0`, locked: see "The narrator's voice" below), model eleven_v4. Run `voiceprint.py` on the takes before choosing one. **Direct the delivery** by the rules in "Directing the voice" below: tags, punctuation and CAPS together, never tags alone. Tags aren't spoken. Pick the tightest take by its pauses (`ffmpeg … silencedetect`), and check its loudness before building on it (step 7).
3. Speed it up a little with `ffmpeg -af atempo=…` (pitch is kept). Choose the tempo so the voice ends about 0.3s before the loop point. Pick the loop first (a multiple of 1.5s, see above), then the tempo: `tempo = last word's end ÷ (loop − 0.3)`. About 1.07–1.13 sounds natural.
4. Transcribe the take with ElevenLabs Scribe for word timings, and keep them in `words.ts`. Every beat and the captions are timed from the words.
5. In `audio.ts`, the voice goes straight to the output; game sound effects stay quiet under it.
6. Scribe often echoes the audio tags as words and stretches the first and last words to the file's edges: drop the tags from `words.ts` and set those edges from `silencedetect`.
7. Check the loudness of the recorded video (`ffmpeg -i <file> -af volumedetect -f null -`): every short should peak around −4 dB. If a take comes out quiet, raise the voice or `OUT_GAIN` in `audio.ts` until it matches.

### Directing the voice

The user's verdict on the 2026-10-16 batch (#37–43): the voice sounded worse than in #23–36, with **the intonation changes missing**. Two things went wrong at once. The scripts were directed flat (fixed by the rules below). And the voice itself had drifted (see "The narrator's voice" above), so from #37 the narrator is Mark. Write every script by these rules:

1. **Use a range of tags, not only calm ones.** Every good directed script mixes high-energy and playful tags with quiet ones: `[excited]`, `[dramatically]`, `[surprised]`, `[chuckles]`, `[sarcastic]`, `[mischievously]`, then `[nervous]`, `[softly]` or `[sad]` for one contrasting beat. **Never `[whispers]`** (see rule 7). The flat batch used almost only `[curious]`, `[softly]` and `[warmly]`.
2. **At least one big lift per script.** Use `[excited]` or `[surprised]` with "!" on the payoff or the most surprising number: *"[excited] and weeks later, he gives birth to a cloud of tiny fish!"*, *"[excited] FIFTY million crabs march to the sea."*
3. **Punctuation carries the intonation.** Use "!" for payoffs, "…" for a suspense pause right before a reveal (*"It looked… then scraped its throat on the sand"*, *"the snow crabs of Alaska's Bering Sea just… disappeared"*), and "?" for a question or a turn (*"The other way?"*). A script of plain full stops reads flat whatever the tags say.
4. **Turn the mood inside a sentence, not only between sentences.** A tag can go mid-sentence: *"[curious] They dunk it in water and rub it between their paws, [surprised] even when the food is already clean."*
5. **Add a human aside.** One `[chuckles]` or `[sarcastic]` beat, or a spoken aside (*"Oh, and the main heart stops beating while it swims."*, *"Dogs do. Cats do."*), makes it sound like a person telling a story.
6. **CAPS on one or two words per sentence**: the twist or the number (*DADS*, *THREE hearts*, *BRIDGES*, *SECONDS*). Not whole phrases.
7. **The quiet beat changes the mood, not the volume. Never use `[whispers]`** (the user, 2026-10-22: whispered parts are hard to hear). In #55 and #57 the whispered line came out 11–12 dB quieter than the rest of the take, and `[softly]` in #58 about 5 dB quieter. For the tender or eerie beat use `[nervous]`, `[sad]` or a slow `[softly]` with "…", once, and come back up after it. After generating, measure that line's loudness (`ffmpeg -ss <start> -t <len> -i take.mp3 -af ebur128 -f null -`): it should be within ~4 dB of the whole take. If it isn't, raise it in `audio.ts` or pick another take.

A good script, for comparison (#23, cleaner wrasse): *"[curious] This fish passed a test your DOG fails. [softly] Scientists put a brown mark on a cleaner wrasse's throat, a spot it could only see in a mirror. [surprised] It looked… then scraped its throat on the sand, trying to rub it off! [dramatically] That's the mirror test. Most animals fail it. [sarcastic] Dogs do. Cats do. [mischievously] But somehow,"*

A flat one (#41 as first voiced): *"[surprised] This fish has HEATED eyes. [curious] Swordfish hunt deep down, where the water is close to freezing. [warmly] So a muscle next to each eye works like a heater, keeping its eyes and brain up to fifteen degrees warmer. [dramatically] Warm eyes follow fast prey up to TEN times better. [mischievously] Forget heated seats."*: calm tags only, no "!" or "…", and no aside.

**Check after generating:** the published takes measure about −23 to −25 LUFS (`ffmpeg -i take.mp3 -af ebur128 -f null -`). On 2026-10-16 every take measured about −30 LUFS. That held for flat scripts and for scripts rewritten by these rules (28 re-voiced takes, −29 to −30.5 LUFS), so low loudness came from the voice drift, not the direction. Run `voiceprint.py` too, and if the takes fail both checks, don't build on them.
