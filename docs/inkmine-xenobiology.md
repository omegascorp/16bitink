# InkMine: life on other worlds

Oct 17, 2026 · @Hayk · research and a first draft of the rules for every creature, plant and people in InkMine

What alien life could really look like, according to research, and how InkMine uses it. The aim is to settle this early, so that every creature, plant and race we add follows the same rules, and so the real science behind them can become shorts and long-form videos later. Facts marked *(unverified)* are from memory, not from a source read during this research. Everything under "Proposed for InkMine" is a draft for @Hayk to accept or change, except where marked as @Hayk's decision (Oct 17, 2026: the habitable-zone rule, crabs on some worlds, Graphite's food web). None of it is built.

## The short version

0. **Life only where water can stay liquid** (@Hayk's rule). Plants and animals live only on worlds in their star's habitable zone. Every other world is lifeless rock, a gas giant or ice. The one exception is an ocean under ice, kept liquid by its planet's tides: research says animals there are possible but sparse, and there are no real plants, because there's no light. Unlike No Man's Sky, most worlds you visit are dead, and that makes the living ones special.
1. **Natural selection works everywhere.** Whatever aliens are made of, if they reproduce with variation, they evolve as Earth's life does (Levin et al., 2017). So every InkMine creature needs a reason to exist: what it eats, what eats it, why it has the shape it has.
2. **The same problems get the same answers.** Earth keeps evolving the same body shapes from unrelated starting points: crabs at least 5 times, eyes 40 to 65 times, multicellular bodies at least 25 times. Physics picks the shape. Chemistry only picks the material. So a crab in the ocean under an ice shell is good science, not a lazy reskin.
3. **The environment changes everything else.** Gravity, light, air pressure, temperature and the energy source decide size, colour, senses and pace. This is where planets stay truly different.
4. **Thinking species share a short list of traits:** a head with the senses next to the brain, something to grab with, social life and a long childhood. A people that builds ships almost certainly evolved on land, in air with plenty of oxygen, because metal needs fire (Balbi & Frank, 2024). The details (how many limbs, how they talk, how they see) are free to vary.
5. **Carbon and water are the most common; later systems try others** (@Hayk's decision). Silicon life in water is close to impossible (Petkowski, Bains & Seager, 2020), and a 2024 review calls liquid methane "extremely implausible" as a home for life. The best-supported other liquid is concentrated sulfuric acid (Bains et al., 2024), which has its own habitable zone, closer to the star.
6. **Almost no air is breathable for the prospector** (@Hayk's idea). A world's air depends on its rocks, its heat and above all its life: free oxygen is made by life. Every world gets its own air, and only one or two let the prospector take the helmet off.

## 1. Where life lives: the habitable zone rule

**@Hayk's rule (Oct 17, 2026):** unlike No Man's Sky, plants and animals live only on worlds in the habitable zone. The rest are rock worlds, gas giants and ice worlds. Life under ice may be the exception, if research allows it.

### What the habitable zone is

The habitable zone is the band of distances from a star where a planet with air could keep liquid water on its surface *(unverified: the standard definition, after Kasting, 1993)*. It is needed for surface life but isn't enough on its own:

- **A planet in the zone can still be dead.** It may be too small to hold air, or have lost its water. Venus sits at the inner edge, Mars at the outer edge *(unverified)*.
- **Moons count.** A giant planet in the zone can have living moons, if each moon is big enough to hold air: roughly Mars's mass, a tenth of Earth's or more. A giant's heat and tides also matter (Heller & Barnes, 2013).
- **Each liquid has its own zone.** Energy-balance calculations put habitable zones for other liquids at other distances and widths (Leitner et al., 2012). Sulfuric acid's zone is closer to the star than water's *(unverified: worked out from its higher boiling range)*.

### Life under the ice: possible, with limits

| Question | Answer | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Can an ocean stay liquid outside the habitable zone? | Yes. Europa and Enceladus have oceans under their ice, warmed by the squeezing of their giant planet's tides | NASA / JHU APL, 2017 |
| Does it have what life needs? | Enceladus's plume carries hydrogen from hot rock reacting with water (2017) and phosphates (2023). No life has been found | NASA / JHU APL, 2017; Postberg et al., 2023 |
| Microbes? | The most plausible case. On Earth, whole ecosystems live on vent chemistry with no sunlight | *(unverified)* |
| Animals? | Possible, but energy is the limit. Animals need an oxidant such as oxygen. Earth's vent animals use oxygen made at the sunlit surface *(unverified)*. One Europa model finds that oxygen made by radiation on the surface ice and carried down could support about 3 million tons of fish-like life (Greenberg, 2010). That's a big "if", resting on the model's assumptions | Greenberg, 2010 |
| Plants? | No: no light. But vent "forests" can look like plants: Earth's tube worms are animals with bacteria inside that feed them | NOAA *(on tube worms)* |
| Where would the life be? | In two places: around vents on the ocean floor, and on the underside of the ice, where chemicals from below meet oxidants from above (Russell et al., 2017) | NASA Astrobiology, 2018 |
| Thinking species? | Unlikely: little energy, and no fire under water anyway (section 4) | — |

**So under-ice worlds work for InkMine:** few creatures, a few big ones at most, no plants but plant-like animal forests, and life clustered at the ice ceiling and at the vents.

### Proposed for InkMine: three kinds of world

| Kind | Where | What lives there | What stops you going deeper |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Living** | A planet or big moon in the habitable zone | Plants and animals on the surface and below | Creatures, so weapons |
| **Under ice / underground** | An ocean or warm brine under a crust, kept liquid by tides | Animals only, sparse; plant-like animal forests; life clustered at the ice ceiling and at vents | Creatures, fewer but tougher; darkness |
| **Dead** | Everywhere else: rock worlds, ice worlds, airless moons | Nothing | The planet itself: heat, cold, falling rock, resonance. The planet's given gear is the gate |

- **Dead worlds are a feature, not a gap.** Players praised Pocket Mine 2 for having no monsters ([inkmine-research.md](inkmine-research.md)). A dead world is pure mining against the planet, a change of pace between living ones.
- **Gas giants aren't landed on.** They fill the sky and hold moons. Skimming their gas for fuel could be a later idea.
- **The suit names the kind on arrival:** "No life signs." Coming down on a living world after two dead ones should feel like a reward.
- **Machines are not life,** so a machine race (section 4) can live anywhere without breaking the rule.

## 2. Convergent evolution: why shapes repeat

On Earth, unrelated lineages keep landing on the same solutions. The palaeontologist Simon Conway Morris built a whole book on it: "the evolutionary routes are many, but the destinations are limited" (*Life's Solution*, 2003). Stephen Jay Gould argued the opposite: replay the tape of life and you'd get something completely different. Today most researchers sit between the two. Broad shapes repeat; exact species don't.

| Shape | How often it evolved on Earth | Why it keeps winning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The crab** (carcinization) | At least 5 times in decapods: twice in true crabs, three times in false crabs, and lost again about 7 times (Wolfe et al., 2021) | A flat, wide armoured body with the tail tucked under. It's good for hiding in cracks and walking sideways, and hard to grab. Nobody is sure which advantage matters most |
| **The camera eye** | Eyes in general: 40 to 65 times (Salvini-Plawen & Mayr, 1977) | Light carries the most information at a distance, so anything that lives in light gets eyes |
| **The multicellular body** | At least 25 times (Grosberg & Strathmann, 2007) | Bigger, divided labour, harder to eat |
| **Powered flight** | 4 times: insects, pterosaurs, birds, bats *(unverified)* | Wherever the air is thick enough |
| **The streamlined swimmer** | Sharks, ichthyosaurs, dolphins *(unverified)* | Water drag gives one best shape |
| **Echolocation** | Bats, toothed whales, oilbirds, swiftlets *(unverified)* | Hunting in the dark |
| **Glowing (bioluminescence)** | Dozens of times, at least 40 by one count *(unverified)* | Lures, signals and camouflage where there's no sunlight |
| **The tree** | Many plant lineages, and a giant fungus-like tree, *Prototaxites*, in the Devonian *(unverified)* | Racing your neighbours for light |
| **The worm** | Many times *(unverified)* | The cheapest way to move through soil |

**What carries over to other worlds.** Arik Kershenbaum (*The Zoologist's Guide to the Galaxy*, 2020) argues that movement, communication and cooperation are "solutions to universal problems", so they appear wherever life evolves. Levin, Scott, Cooper and West ("Darwin's aliens", 2017) say the same holds "whether aliens are made of carbon or silicon, have DNA or XNA". They predict that complex aliens are built in layers, like ours: simple parts that joined into bigger wholes (cells into bodies, bodies into colonies).

### Proposed for InkMine: the shape rule

- **Every creature starts from a convergent shape:** crab, worm, eye-stalk, wing, swimmer, tree, glow lure, trap. The planet then changes its size, material, colour and behaviour.
- **Crabs on some worlds, not all** (@Hayk's decision). The crab was @Hayk's idea and it's the best hook in this research, but it stays special by not being everywhere. Each world's crab looks different (armour of hematite on Talus, a blind glowing one under Umbra's ice), but it's always recognisably a crab. The field guide counts them: "Crab-form · the 3rd world I've seen it on."
- **A "Shapes" page in the field guide** lists the convergent shapes and which planets you've seen each one on. It fills in as you travel. It's a light collection that rewards exploring and needs no new mechanics.

## 3. What the environment changes

| Factor | What it does | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The star's colour** | Plant pigments tune to the light they get. Around hot white-blue stars, plants may absorb blue. Around orange stars, red-orange. Around red dwarfs, near-infrared, and they could look black because they keep all the scarce visible light. Blue plants are unlikely anywhere | Kiang et al., 2007; Lehmer et al., 2021 |
| **A different pigment** | Early Earth may have been purple: retinal (the pigment in salt-loving microbes) may have come before chlorophyll | DasSarma & Schwieterman, 2018 |
| **Gravity** | Low gravity allows tall, thin bodies and plants. High gravity means short, squat, many-legged bodies. Weight grows faster than strength as size goes up (the square-cube law) | *(unverified)* standard biomechanics |
| **Air pressure** | Thick air makes flying easy and big fliers possible; thin air makes it hard | *(unverified)* |
| **No sunlight at all** | Life can run on chemical energy: hydrothermal vents, or water split by natural radioactivity in rock. On Earth, a single-species ecosystem lives 2.8 km down a South African gold mine on exactly that | *(unverified)* Lin et al., 2006; Chivian et al., 2008 |
| **Under an ice shell** | Saturn's moon Enceladus has hydrogen in its plume, the sign of hot rock reacting with water on its ocean floor (2017), and phosphates (2023). These are the ingredients for vent life, though no life has been found | NASA / JHU APL, 2017; Postberg et al., 2023 |
| **Heat** | On Earth, the hottest-living microbe grows at about 122 °C. Beyond that, carbon-and-water chemistry falls apart | *(unverified)* |
| **Cold** | Chemistry slows down. Life in liquid methane would be extremely slow | Rahm et al., 2020 (see section 6) |
| **Tidal locking** | Planets close to red dwarfs keep one face to the star: a hot day side, a frozen night side, and a mild ring between them | *(unverified)* |

### Air: every world breathes something different

@Hayk (Oct 17, 2026): the air should differ on every planet, almost none should suit the prospector, and one or two can be a twist where they can breathe. Rocky in *Project Hail Mary* breathes ammonia, which no human could.

**What research says:**

- **Oxygen is made by life.** Free oxygen doesn't last in an atmosphere unless something keeps making it. On Earth, that's photosynthesis that splits water. That's why oxygen is a sign of life when astronomers look at exoplanets *(unverified)*.
- **Earth's own air was unbreathable for most of its history.** Oxygen only built up after the Great Oxygenation Event, about 2.4 billion years ago (DasSarma & Schwieterman, 2018), and reached today's level much later *(unverified)*.
- **Not all light-eating life makes oxygen.** The purple retinal pigment that may have coloured early Earth captures light without splitting water, so it releases no oxygen *(unverified)*. A purple living world can still be airless for us.
- **Dead worlds have whatever their rocks and volcanoes give:** carbon dioxide, nitrogen, sulfur gases, methane, or nothing on small moons (Heller & Barnes).
- **The prospector breathes oxygen.** Section 4: a people that smelts metal almost certainly evolved in oxygen-rich air. So the prospector's air exists only where life like theirs made it.

**Proposed for InkMine:**

- **The suit is always needed,** except on the twist worlds. It's already built that way: life support is fed with ice, which the suit splits into air.
- **Each world names its air** in the arrival line: "Air: carbon dioxide, sulfur. Not breathable."
- **Creatures breathe their world's air** or none at all, and some breathe what would kill you. The field guide's scan line includes it.
- **Other peoples need their own air.** The Lamplighters' domes hold an air the prospector can't breathe either, so meetings happen suited, and the inside of their domes stays a mystery for a while.
- **The twist:** one world where the helmet comes off. Life support stops draining, the helmet glass is gone from the drawing, and the suit says it quietly: "Air: breathable. I don't understand." Section 7 proposes Cardia.

### Proposed for InkMine: an energy source for every planet

Each planet names where its life gets energy. That decides where the creatures are, which is a level-design tool.

- **Sunlit planets:** plants on the surface, grazers and hunters near them, fewer creatures as you dig down.
- **Under-ice and underground worlds (Graphite, Umbra):** life lives on chemical energy from the deep, so **the deeper you go, the more and fiercer the creatures.** This gives a real reason for the existing rule that weapons are the depth gate.
- **Dead worlds:** no creatures; the danger is the planet.

## 4. Thinking creatures

### What Earth's smart animals have in common

Intelligence evolved separately in primates, whales and dolphins, elephants, crows and parrots, and octopuses. Kershenbaum: "intelligence evolves all the time to fit specific needs". What most of these groups share:

| Trait | Why it matters | The exception |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A head**: brain, eyes and mouth at the front | A moving animal meets the world with its front end, so the senses sit there and the brain sits next to them | — |
| **Something to grab with**: hands, a trunk, a beak and feet, arms | You can only use tools if you can hold them | Dolphins: smart, almost no tools |
| **Social life** | Keeping track of others, cooperating and deceiving them is a hard problem that rewards big brains | Octopuses are mostly solitary |
| **A long life and a long childhood** | Time to learn and to be taught | Octopuses live 1 to 2 years |
| **A varied diet** | Food that needs finding, opening or planning | — |

The octopus is the useful counterexample: smart along a completely different road, with most of its neurons in its arms *(unverified)*. Aliens can be smart in ways that don't look like us.

### From smart to spacefaring

- **Fire needs oxygen.** Open-air burning, and with it metalwork, needs an atmosphere of at least about 18% oxygen (Balbi & Frank, 2024). A species that evolved underwater or in thin air could be brilliant and still never smelt metal. So the races that build ships evolved on land, in oxygen-rich air.
- **Intelligence may not be a fluke.** The old "hard steps" model (Carter, 1983) said humans needed a chain of improbable accidents. A 2025 reassessment argues instead that each step waited for Earth's environment to open the next "window", so intelligent life may be more common than thought (Mills et al., 2025).

### Likely similar to us, likely different

| Likely similar | Free to differ |
| --- | --- |
| Bilateral body with a front and a back | Number of limbs: four is an accident of the fish our ancestors came from *(unverified)* |
| A head with the main senses | Senses: infrared, electric fields, magnetism, sonar |
| Eyes, if they evolved in light | How they talk: sound, colour changes on the skin (like cuttlefish), light, electric pulses (like electric fish, which Kershenbaum points to), smell |
| Manipulators | How the brain is laid out: one brain, or spread out as in the octopus |
| Social, with a language that combines small units | Life cycle: metamorphosis, castes as in ants, more than two sexes or none |
| Medium-to-large size: brains need room *(unverified)* | Pace of life, sense of time, number base (counting on six fingers gives base 12) |
| Evolved on land, with oxygen (for metalworkers) | Symmetry (fivefold, as in starfish), colours, body temperature |

**One more possibility:** life that builds machines may in time *become* machines. Some astronomers argue that most old civilizations would now be post-biological *(unverified: Steven Dick, 2003)*.

### Proposed for InkMine: grounding the races

- **The Lamplighters** fit the research well and only need a reason for their details. Proposal: they evolved on the dim, dark side of a world circling a red dwarf, near the mild ring between day and night. That gives:
  - **Huge eyes, and lanterns** that glow deep red, the colour their eyes see best. Our white light hurts them a little, a small moment in a scene.
  - **Black plants** on their home world (Kiang), which could appear in their inscriptions.
  - **Knot writing you read by touch.** A species from the dark would invent writing you can feel. Earth really had one: the Inca khipu, records kept in knotted cords *(unverified)*. This is already their writing; now it has a reason.
  - **Their home stays offstage.** The dark world shows up only in the Lamplighters' stories, once you're fluent. (The prospector has a home too, but a wild wormhole took the memory of where it is: finding it in the sky is the game's goal. See "The way home" in the design spec.)
- **The prospector's species:** @Hayk wants them much less human, and not every race humanoid. See section 5.
- **No natives think on Graphite.** A moon too small to hold air, whose life runs on chemical energy deep down, is the wrong place for a thinking species to evolve. This matches the story already: the Lamplighters came there to mine.
- **Later races:** a mix of the possibilities, one per planet or system. Some ideas:
  - **A crab-shaped people.** The claws are their hands, and the joke writes itself.
  - **A hive people** of castes, like ants or naked mole-rats, where you trade with a colony, not a person.
  - **An ocean people** on a living sea world (not under ice, where there's too little energy) who are clever but can't make fire. They ask you for metal because they can't smelt it, and pay in knowledge.
  - **A people who talk in skin colour,** like cuttlefish. Their language is shown as moving patterns, not glyphs.
  - **Machines whose makers are gone.** This echoes the Aster crew's reprinting, InkMine's quiet horror.

## 5. How aliens could look: science and fiction

@Hayk (Oct 17, 2026): the prospector can be much more different from us, and not every race has to be humanoid.

### Why most fictional aliens look like people

- **Budget and actors.** Film and TV aliens were mostly people in suits or make-up, so they stood on two legs with a face. *Babylon 5* set out to have non-humanoid aliens and fell back on humanoids because of cost. *Star Trek* later explained its humanoids in-story with an ancient race that seeded them *(unverified)*.
- **Faces we can read.** Audiences connect with figures that resemble them.

**For InkMine:** a drawn game has no budget or actor limit. Only readability is left: the player must see at a glance where the prospector is looking, what it's holding and how it feels.

### What science and fiction got right

| --- | --- | --- |
| *Project Hail Mary* (Andy Weir, 2021) | **Rocky**: five limbs around a central carapace, no eyes, senses by sound and vibration, talks in musical chords, breathes ammonia under high pressure. Weir kept spreadsheets to make the physics consistent | A body nothing like ours can still be the most loved character. Personality comes from voice and gesture |
| *Arrival* (2016), from Ted Chiang's story | **Heptapods**: seven limbs, radial symmetry, circular ink writing *(unverified)* | The language can be the alien part. Close to the Lamplighters' knot writing |
| *Mission of Gravity* (Hal Clement, 1953) | **Mesklinites**: small, long, many-legged, from a world of crushing gravity, afraid of heights *(unverified)* | Gravity shapes the body and the mind |
| *A Fire Upon the Deep* (Vernor Vinge, 1992) | **Tines**: dog-like packs of four to eight sharing one mind through sound *(unverified)* | One person can be several bodies |
| *Children of Time* (Adrian Tchaikovsky, 2015) | Thinking **spiders**; octopuses in the sequel *(unverified)* | Intelligence on a body plan far from ours, with different senses |
| *Expedition* (Wayne Barlowe, 1990), *Alien Planet* (2005) | **Darwin IV**: no animal has eyes, outer ears, hair or jaws, on purpose | "Truly alien" is possible, but one reviewer called the animals "dinosaurs designed by a committee" and doubted the ecology worked. Strangeness needs a reason |
| *Alien Worlds* (Netflix, 2020) | Four planets built from Earth's rules: fliers in thick air on a heavy world, pentapods on a tidally locked one, a two-sun world, robots of a dying civilization | The same method as this doc, done as a documentary |

### What our prospector's body must do

From the research (section 4): a front end with the senses, something to grab with, evolved on land in oxygen-rich air. From the game:

- **Hold and aim the drill** in four directions, and push into rock with it.
- **Wear a suit and helmet** whose three looks per gear mark still read (the gear table in the design spec).
- **Fly the jetpack, climb ladders, fit tunnels** one or two tiles high.
- **Show where it's looking and how it feels** at sprite size. The eyes look where the drill points, and that should stay.

## 6. Does all life have to be carbon?

Carbon is the best bet because it forms four strong bonds, long chains and rings, and stays reactive in water. The real question is the pair: **a building element and a liquid** for chemistry to happen in.

| Chemistry | How plausible | What research says | Where it could be |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Carbon + water** | Proven (us) | Water remains the most likely liquid for life (Bains et al., 2024) | Living worlds in the habitable zone, oceans under ice |
| **Carbon + water with ammonia** | Plausible as a mix *(unverified)* | Ammonia works as antifreeze in water. On its own it is unlikely to be liquid, so unlikely as a solvent (Bains et al., 2024) | Oceans under ice |
| **Carbon + sulfuric acid** | Debated, but stronger than expected | Concentrated sulfuric acid "meets all the criteria for a solvent for life" (Bains et al., 2024). 19 of 20 amino acids keep their backbone in concentrated sulfuric acid, the stuff of Venus's clouds (Seager et al., 2024). Sara Seager: that "doesn't mean there is life on Venus" | Floating ecosystems in acid clouds |
| **Carbon + liquid methane** (−180 °C) | Extremely unlikely | Too little dissolves at such cold (Bains et al., 2024). Proposed "azotosome" membranes for Titan turned out not to self-assemble (Rahm et al., 2020) | None: methane lakes stay on dead ice worlds |
| **Silicon + water** | Very unlikely | Silicon in water locks up as silica (sand, glass), so it can only be a rare helper element (Petkowski, Bains & Seager, 2020) | Nowhere as a base, but see below |
| **Silicon + sulfuric acid** | Speculative, the best case for silicon | The same review: sulfuric acid supports much more silicon chemistry than water | The acid-cloud worlds |
| **Silicon + liquid nitrogen** | Very unlikely | Silicon compounds barely dissolve (same review) | — |
| **Silicon in Earth's life** | Real | Diatoms and sponges build glass skeletons; engineered bacterial enzymes have made carbon-silicon bonds *(unverified: Kan et al., 2016)* | Armour, everywhere |
| **Machine life** | Plausible as a descendant of builders | See section 4 | An old system |

### Proposed for InkMine: carbon and water first, other chemistries later

@Hayk's decision: carbon + water is the most common, and later star systems explore other options. Each living world still sits in the habitable zone **of its own liquid** (Leitner et al., 2012), so the rule holds. The options, from best supported to fringe:

| Where | Chemistry | What that means in play |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The first system** (the current 10 planets) | Carbon + water, on living worlds and under ice | Familiar rules first. Silicon appears only as glass, in rock (Prism) |
| **A later system: acid** | Carbon + sulfuric acid, and the first part-silicon life, in the acid's own zone, closer to its star than water's | The best-supported alternative. The surface is too hot; life floats in the acid clouds; the first scan reading "silicon" is a moment |
| **A later system: ammonia-water** | Carbon in a cold ammonia-water mix, on a world farther out than water's zone | Cold, slow, pale life; plausible as a mix, not as pure ammonia |
| **A later system: machines** | No chemistry: machine life | Not flora or fauna, so allowed on any world. Makers gone; ties into reprinting |
| **Fringe: liquid methane** | Life in methane lakes at −180 °C | Research calls it extremely unlikely, so if it's used, the game says so: the suit reads "this shouldn't exist", and it becomes a mystery, not a fact |

**Showing it in the game:**

- **The scan line.** Every field guide entry starts with one line: "Carbon · water · chemical energy". The first time a line doesn't say "carbon", the suit says so out loud.
- **The suit's comments** take it seriously: "Silicon. That shouldn't work. The acid must be doing it."
- **No new mechanics needed.** The chemistry shows in the art, the field guide and the planet's hazards, not in a new system to learn.

## 7. The first system's planets, through this lens

A first pass over the current ten under the habitable-zone rule. Mechanics stay; who lives where changes.

**Built Oct 17, 2026** as the first star system (design spec, "The first star system"; `logic/starSystem.ts`): this layout, around an orange dwarf, with the giants named Sepia (ringed) and Indigo (ice giant), and Pumice as Indigo's small porous moon rather than a planet of its own.

**A layout that fits the rule.** A habitable zone has room for only a few planets: our Sun's has Earth, with Venus and Mars at its edges *(unverified)*. TRAPPIST-1, a red dwarf, has three or four in its zone *(unverified)*. The way to fit more living worlds is a **ringed giant in the habitable zone with big moons**: Graphite already has a ringed planet in its sky. From the star outwards:

| Orbit | World | Kind | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Closest | **Ember** | Dead | Too hot: lava, no water |

**What the dead worlds lose.** Four planets lose their creatures and giants, and weapons stop mattering there. Two ways to handle it:

1. **Hazards only** (recommended). Each dead world's gate is its given gear (coolant foam on Ember, the thermal lance on Rime, the tuning fork on Prism). It's the cleanest reading of the rule and the biggest change of pace.
2. **Hazards plus machines.** Old automated miners or guardians left by someone else keep weapons useful. This works under the rule, since machines aren't life, but it uses up the machine race's surprise early.

## What this changes in the design spec

- **The field guide's xenobiology notebook** stops being "clearly fiction". Each entry is invented but grounded: its scan line (chemistry and energy), its shape, and a note in the prospector's voice.
- **The ten planets' table** gets a "kind" column (living, under ice, dead), and Pumice, Rime, Ember and Prism lose their creatures if this is accepted. Umbra becomes an under-ice moon.
- **No Earth in the game.** InkMine has no humans, and the prospector is lost far from anything they know, so the notebook never compares creatures with Earth. Its notes are what the prospector sees and works out. The Earth science lives in the videos and in this doc.
