An elimination tournament with real fish, MrBeast's "last one standing" shape: 100 fish enter, five rounds, one winner, and a counter of fish left on screen the whole time. Every pass and every fail comes from a published number (the line-up, counts and 5-minute timer are staging, declared on screen), stamped **MEASURED**, **ESTIMATED** or **REPORTED**; where no study exists we say so ("no study shows it can"). Nobody gets hurt: a fish that fails gets an OUT stamp and swims off the stage.

- **Format:** 1920×1080, 30 fps, not looping. Target about 7:00 (~880 words plus a beat of silence before every verdict; padding to 8:00 would cost retention), the end card holding 5 s after the last word.
- **Voice:** the locked narrator, *Mark – Natural Conversations* (`UgBBYS2sOqTuMpoF3BR0`, `eleven_v4`), as the tournament host. Directed by "Directing the voice" in `docs/shorts-playbook.md`: a new mood every sentence, "!" and "…", an aside per block.
- **Sound:** a buzzer and a stamp thump on every OUT; a soft chime on every pass; a counter tick as the number drops; a crowd "ooh" on the shocks; a drum bed that tightens round by round; silence before each verdict.
- **Captions:** an `.srt` from the word timings, uploaded as CC.
- **Not Squid Game:** no doll, no masked guards, no tracksuits, no 3-digit jersey numbers, no music from the show, never the name. Our own look: a pen-and-ink arena tank, contestant cards, stamps.

## Why this shape

- **The open loop runs to the end.** Our ladders keep about a third of viewers (31–36%) because the top step is guessable. Here "who's left?" stays open until the final verdict.
- **A number on screen at every step:** the FISH LEFT counter, 100 → 8 → 5 → 4 → 2 → 1.
- **Viewers pick a fish in the first minute** (the eight finalists' cards), so every round matters to someone. Pinned: *"Which fish did you pick, and when did it go out? 🐟"*
- **The emotional route:** joke (the squid) → shock (the great white out first) → myth-bust (sailfish) → heartbreak (the icefish) → near miss (the eel, scraping through) → loss at home (your goldfish) → reveal (the tiny fish's 66 days) → irony (the land-walker beaten by a rain puddle) → triumph.
- **Arguable lines (comment test):** "The great white is out. In round one." / "Is climbing out of the water allowed? …We'll allow it." / "The fish that walks on land… can't handle a rain puddle."

### 2 · Round 1: Stop swimming (1:10–2:20)

> [dramatically] Round one is simple. Stop swimming. For five minutes. [chuckles] Easy, right? [nervous] Not for everyone. Some fish can only breathe while they move: water has to rush in through the mouth and out over the gills. [surprised] So when the tunas stop… skipjack, yellowfin, bluefin… ninety of them are out! [shocked] And then… the great white. [dramatically] The great white shark is OUT. In round one! [curious] Scientists class it as a shark that has to keep swimming to breathe, so a great white that stops… is in trouble. [sarcastic] The mako? Same problem, say the books. Out. [warmly] And before you type it: no, not every shark has to swim. A nurse shark can lie on the bottom and pump water itself. [relieved] Everyone else pumps water over their own gills… and just waits. [excited] Eight fish left!

*Moods: epic → playful → dread → mechanism → shock → bigger shock → explanation → deadpan → reassurance → relief → relief.*

*A big FREEZE sign over the tank; a 5:00 timer starts. A water-flow diagram on one fish: arrows in at the mouth, out at the gills. The school (five kinds of tuna mixed through it; a small name label flashes on a skipjack, a yellowfin and a bluefin as Mark names them) slows, and ninety OUT stamps rattle across them like hail, the counter spinning 100 → 10. Silence. The great white glides in, slows, stops; it looks at camera. OUT (biggest stamp of the video), crowd gasp, its tag "classed as must-keep-swimming · ESTIMATED". The mako: OUT, tag "ESTIMATED". A cut-in card: a nurse shark resting on sand, "SOME SHARKS CAN REST · REPORTED". The eight survivors hang still, eyes darting. Counter 8.*

Sources: ram ventilation, skipjack (Brown & Muir 1970, J Fish Res Board Can 27:1637); skipjack, yellowfin and bigeye ram-ventilate (Bushnell et al. 1990, NOAA SWFSC); *Thunnus* tunas are obligate ram ventilators, while mackerels only ram-ventilate at speed, so no mackerel or bonito in the school (Wegner et al. 2013, J Morphol); evidence tag "TUNAS · must keep swimming to breathe · ESTIMATED"; white shark classed as an obligate ram ventilator from its anatomy, never tested by stopping an adult (Semmens et al. 2013, Sci Rep 3:1471; Britannica), so ESTIMATED. Mako lacks cheek-muscle pumping (Live Science; Wegner et al. 2010, J Morphol, gill morphometrics of ram-ventilating sharks, not opened). Bony fish pump water with their gill covers (textbook; no single study cited on screen). Nurse shark buccal pumps and rests (New England Aquarium). Only ~20–24 of ~500 shark species are thought obligate, and the list is being revised (a 2025 grey reef shark study). "5 minutes", "90 tuna" and the 100-fish line-up are our staging; nobody stopped a live great white.

*Moods: hype → sly → info → deadpan → surprise → laugh → warm → dread.*

Sources: Marras et al. 2015, Integr Comp Biol 55:719 (accelerometers and video on hunting sailfish: ~7 m/s ≈ 15.7 mph burst, not above 10 m/s ≈ 22 mph); Svendsen et al. 2016 (muscle estimate 8.3 m/s ≈ 18.6 mph). Svendsen et al. 2016 dropped (journal unconfirmed, not on screen). Marras measured hunting bursts only, so "never over 22" is limited to the recorded hunts.

### 4 · Round 2: Hot water (3:00–4:20)

> [dramatically] Round two: we warm the water steadily… to a hundred degrees Fahrenheit. [quickly] That's thirty-eight Celsius. [dramatically] Lose your balance, you're out. [nervous] Seventy-two degrees… [surprised] and the cod is out! A North Atlantic fish, [sarcastic] it's basically a hot tub. [sad] Fifty-seven degrees… wait, that's already gone? [sorrowful] The icefish. It lives in water below freezing, its blood is see-through… and a mild fifty-seven is too much for it. [nervous] Around seventy-nine: the salmon, out! [dramatically] Ninety-nine… a hundred… [gasps] the eel's upper limit is about a hundred point four. [excited] It scrapes through by less than HALF a degree! [curious] And the goldfish? A goldfish kept in warm water stays upright up to a hundred and ten. [chuckles] Your pet is tougher than you think. Five fish left.

*Moods: epic → tense → shock → joke → confusion → grief → shock → suspense → gasp → relief → curious → warm.*

*The thermometer climbs on screen, every reading in both units (°F big, °C under it), and every evidence tag the same; the water tints from blue to orange; heat shimmer, sweat drops. At 72 °F (22 °C) the cod wobbles, tips: OUT, tag "loses balance ~72 °F (22 °C) · MEASURED". Rewind gag: the needle jumps back to 57 °F (14 °C); the icefish is already lying on its side, pale, glassy; OUT (a soft, sad stamp, no buzzer; one piano note), tag "~57 °F (14 °C) · MEASURED". Salmon at ~79 °F (26 °C): OUT, "ESTIMATED". At 99–100 the eel trembles, the needle stops at 100.0, a ruler pops up showing 100.0 against 100.4: a green tick, crowd cheer; tag "upper lethal limit 100.4 °F (38 °C), not loss of balance · MEASURED, a coin flip". The goldfish fans its fins, unbothered; tag "warm-kept: loses balance ~110 °F (43.6 °C) · MEASURED". Pupfish, rivulus and mudskipper float through (small tags: "~109 °F (42.7 °C) · MEASURED"; "~105 °F (40.5 °C) · MEASURED, derived from the paper"; "P. argentilineatus climbs out at ~106 °F (41.3 °C) · MEASURED"). Counter 5.*

Sources: cod CTmax 21.4–23.0 °C (PeerJ 2019, doi 10.7717/peerj.7784); icefish *Chaenocephalus aceratus* CTmax ~14 °C (Beers & Sidell 2011, Physiol Biochem Zool 84:353, value of 14 °C vs 17 °C for red-blooded *Notothenia coriiceps* seen in later papers citing it; original not opened); icefish blood has no haemoglobin (Kim et al. 2019, Nat Ecol Evol); Antarctic sea water about −1.9 °C. Salmon ~26 °C (indirect, ESTIMATED; the claim is only "far below 100 °F"). European eel ultimate upper lethal 38 °C; CTmax 33–39 °C depending on acclimation (Sadler 1979, J Fish Biol 15:499; abstract only). The eel pass uses the lethal limit, so it is a coin flip by design. Goldfish CTmax 30.8–43.6 °C by acclimation, 43.6 at 35 °C acclimation (Ford & Beitinger 2005, abstract only); the goldfish passes only if warm-kept, and we say so. Pupfish 41.9–42.7 °C (Desert Fishes Council Proc. XLI). Rivulus loss of equilibrium = 15.5 °C above a 25 °C acclimation (~40.5 °C) and 11.5 °C above 30 °C (~41.5 °C), a ~1 °C/min ramp (Biol Lett 2015, PMC4650182, read this pass; absolute values are our arithmetic, Fig 1 not seen). Barred mudskipper (*P. argentilineatus*) held at 32 °C climbs out of the water at 41.3 °C: an escape, not a loss of balance (JCU). The published tests are fast steady ramps (0.3–2 °C/min), not hour-long holds: "lose your balance" is what CTmax measures.

No abrupt fresh-to-sea test found, so the eel pass is REPORTED and the weakest in the video; the line is "allowed", not "measured".

*Moods: epic → dread → surprise → laugh → curious → sly → deadpan → tension → sad → sad → hype → curious → shock → epic.*

Cut-in: a lumpy mud cocoon with "Zzz", card "MONTHS TO YEARS IN A COCOON · REPORTED". The eel wriggles over wet grass, stops: OUT, tag "no 24 h study · REPORTED". Sources: flying fish 45 s (Guinness, NHK 2008). Lungfish aestivation 7–8 months typical in the wild (PMC3745453), "4 years" REPORTED; the on-screen tag says only "months to years". Rivulus: at least 66 days in a damp-mud lab microcosm, emaciated (Taylor 1990, Florida Scientist 53:239, quoted in Taylor 2012 PMC3501094).

[excited] Brine first! This mudskipper is Periophthalmus minutus: twice as salty as the sea, not one died. Fine! [surprised] The rivulus: found in the wild up to twice the sea, and in a lab it got used to more than THREE times. [curious] Now… fresh water. [nervous] The rivulus is found in fresh water too, no problem. [gasps] And the mudskipper… [sorrowful] in fresh water, half of them were dead within four days. [sarcastic] The fish that walks on land… can't handle a rain puddle. [dramatically] The mudskipper is OUT!

*Moods: epic → tension → hype → relief → shock → curious → tension → gasp → grief → cheeky → climax.*

*Two tanks side by side, VS card. A salinity gauge in each, with the sea marked; brine poured: both gauges to "2x · 70 ppt", two ticks; a small aside under the rivulus: "lab-adapted to 114 ppt, 3x+". Then fresh water runs into both tanks. Rivulus: tick. The mudskipper's tank, slow push-in, a 4-day calendar flipping; on "half of them", half its silhouette fades out; OUT. Its tag: "P. minutus · half died in 4 days in fresh water · MEASURED"; a small line: "other mudskippers differ".*

Sources: *Periophthalmus minutus*: no deaths in 100% and 200% sea water, half died in fresh water within 4 days (Itoki et al. 2012, Zool Sci 29:444, abstract confirmed this pass; the Frontiers 2018 review could not be matched to it). Name the species on screen: other mudskippers differ. Rivulus: collected at 0–70 ppt in the wild, "could adapt to 114 ppt" in lab transfers (Taylor 2012, PMC3501094, read this pass). The tank is set at 2x so both fish have a number; the mudskipper has no 3x data. Fresh water: the rivulus is wild-collected at 0 ppt. The mudskipper's loss is species-specific and the on-screen tag says so; "rain puddle" replaces "tap water" (chlorine is not tested).

### 8 · The winner (7:40–8:10)

> [excited] The winner… the mangrove rivulus! [warmly] A fish no longer than your thumb, [surprised] that lives in crab holes and rotting logs, [curious] takes in air through its skin and fins when the water's gone… [dramatically] and beat a great white shark!
