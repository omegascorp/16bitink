# Long-form #3: "Every ocean animal that could swallow you… only one actually could"

**Status:** scripted and voiced 2026-10-26.

A narrated ladder of the ocean's biggest mouths, from the goliath grouper (8 ft) to the sperm whale (60 ft). At every step, the same test: can this animal swallow an average person whole? Every animal gets a verdict stamp. The twist at the end: only the sperm whale's throat is big enough, and there's no record of it, or any whale, ever swallowing a person.

- **Format:** 1920×1080, 30 fps, not looping. Target about 5:00 (~800 words), with the end card holding 5 s after the last word.
- **Voice:** the locked narrator, *Mark – Natural Conversations* (`UgBBYS2sOqTuMpoF3BR0`, `eleven_v4`), directed by "Directing the voice" in `docs/shorts-playbook.md`. One section per take, four takes each.
- **Sound:** the voice; a low bed that grows as the mouths get bigger; the game's gulp and bite effects; a stamp thump on every verdict; a ruler tick as each animal's length is measured.
- **Captions:** an `.srt` built from the word timings, uploaded as CC (not burned in).

## Why this shape

- **It's our winning short, stretched.** Every section opens on one giant open mouth, the frame behind all three narrated-swallow breakouts (71–82% stayed to watch).
- **Proof it travels (research, 2026-10-26):** Occam's *Every Animal That Could Swallow You (Only One Actually Can)* got 276K views in 3 weeks on a 2.6K-subscriber channel; its earlier version without the "(Only One…)" twist got 21K. *8 Ocean Bites Ranked. The Last One Would Crush You* (Benny Goes Wild, 2.6K subs) got 113K in 4 days. *Scientists Finally Identified the Predator That ATE a Great White Whole* got 415K in a month. Our take is ocean-only, every claim sourced, and ends on a twist that is true.
- **A topic in the news:** Science News (2026-09-10), writing about the film *Whalefall*: no whale has ever been documented eating a person, and the sperm whale is the only one where "maybe" holds. Use "whalefall" in the tags.
- **MrBeast's stair-step** (`docs/longform-playbook.md`): the animals go up in length, 8 ft → 60 ft, with a size ruler at every step; the low steps go by fast, and the top steps get the most time.
- **One repeated test, so every section pays off the title:** "the shoulder test". An average man's shoulders are about 16 inches (40 cm) across. Each animal's throat goes up against that bar, and a stamp lands: NO, NO, NO… then COULD.
- **Open loops:** (1) the kayaker in the cold open, closed at the humpback; (2) "only one actually could, and it's not the one you think", closed at the sperm whale; (3) "the one that HAS eaten people", the crocodile, as the re-hook after the game mention.
- **Arguable lines (comment test):** "Honestly? Jaws lied to you." and "You're not squid." Pinned question: *"Which one scares you most: the great white, the croc or the sperm whale? 🦈🐊🐋"*.

## Picture

- **"You" stand at the left of every shot**: an ink silhouette of an average man (5 ft 9 in / 1.75 m), always to scale, so every animal is sized against a person.
- **A size ruler** along the bottom, in feet with metres under it, grows as the animals do (a camera zoom out per step, like the food chain's).
- **The shoulder test**: a 16-inch (40 cm) bar labelled "your shoulders" slides up to each animal's throat, drawn as a ring. If the ring is smaller, the bar bounces off; a red **NO** stamp thumps on. For the sperm whale, the bar slips through and the stamp reads **COULD** (in yellow), then a small "…but never has" under it.
- **A stamp card** in the top-right corner collects the verdicts: eight small mouths, each with its stamp.
- **A name card** for every animal: "THE GOLIATH GROUPER · 8 ft · 800 lb" (US units first, metric under).
- Something new comes on screen every 10–15 s; every animal is introduced mouth-first.

## Script

Each section gives the line as it will be voiced, the mood of each sentence, then what's on screen. Times are targets; `timeline.ts` will read the real ones off the words.

### 0 · Cold open (0:00–0:30)

> [excited] In 2025, a humpback whale swallowed a kayaker. [surprised] Kayak and ALL! [nervous] For a few seconds, he was inside its mouth… [chuckles] and then it spat him out, like a bad grape. [curious] So here's the question: which animal in the ocean could actually swallow YOU… and keep you? [dramatically] We're going from the smallest mouth that's tried it, to the biggest throat on Earth. [mischievously] And only ONE of them actually could. It's not the one you think.

*Moods: thrill → shock → dread → silly → curious → epic → sly.*

*Frame 1: a humpback's mouth surging up out of the water around a yellow kayak, mid-gulp (gulp SFX). It closes; a beat; it opens and the kayaker pops back out (a "pft" spit sound). On "YOU", a hard cut to "you": the ink silhouette, alone, centred. On "smallest mouth… biggest throat", a fast fly-by of the eight mouths in a row, small to huge (the stamp card's thumbnails). On "only ONE", seven mouths grey out and one stays dark, unnamed.*

Sources: Adrián Simancas, Strait of Magellan, Chile, 8 Feb 2025, engulfed with his kayak for a few seconds, uninjured (Maritime Executive; Fox 9).

### 1 · You (0:30–0:45)

> [warmly] First, the meal. That's you. An average guy is about five foot nine… [sarcastic] with shoulders about SIXTEEN inches across. [dramatically] Anything that wants to swallow you whole has to get THOSE down its throat. Let's test it.

*Moods: cosy → teasing → serious → game on.*

*The silhouette turns side-on; a height bar: "5 ft 9 in / 1.75 m". Shoulder bar draws across his shoulders: "16 in / 40 cm". On "down its throat", the bar lifts off and becomes the test gauge in the corner.*

### 2 · Goliath grouper, 8 ft (0:45–1:15)

> [excited] Up first: the goliath grouper! Up to eight hundred POUNDS, with a mouth like a trash can lid. [surprised] In 2014, off Florida, one swallowed a four-foot shark in a single bite! [nervous] And big ones have been seen following divers… [curious] But there's no record of one ever attacking a person. [softly] A four-foot shark is skinny. You're not. [dramatically] Verdict: NO.

*Moods: hype → shock → creepy → relief → teasing → final.*

*The grouper's mouth fills the screen, opening and closing, then the camera pulls back to it beside "you". Name card. On "2014", a hooked blacktip on a line, and the grouper takes it in one gulp (the goliath short's art). On "following divers", the grouper drifts behind a diver's silhouette. The shoulder test: the bar bounces off its throat. NO stamp.*

Sources: up to 2.5 m and 363 kg (800 lb); the August 2014 Bonita Springs video of a goliath taking a hooked ~4 ft blacktip in one bite; "stalked divers", "unsuccessful ambushes", no documented attacks (Florida Museum; Time).

### 3 · Tiger shark, 18 ft (1:15–1:40)

> [excited] The tiger shark, though? It'll eat ANYTHING. [surprised] One in the Red Sea had two cans, a plastic bottle and two burlap sacks inside it! [sarcastic] They call it the garbage can of the sea. [curious] But even a tiger shark bites first and asks questions later. [dramatically] Verdict: NO.

*Moods: thrill → disgust-laugh → sarcasm → wary → final.*

*The tiger's mouth opens in close-up, then it's beside "you" (it's three "yous" long). On "two cans…", the items pop out one at a time in a cut-away of its belly, each with a "ding". The bar bounces. NO.*

Sources: females over 5.5 m (18 ft), over 900 kg (2,000 lb); the Red Sea female's stomach contents (Florida Museum). The "license plate" story is unsourced: don't use it.

### 4 · Great white, 20 ft (1:40–2:05)

> [dramatically] And the one from Jaws. A great white can reach about TWENTY feet… [nervous] and it has more recorded fatal attacks than any other shark. [curious] But watch how it eats a seal: it BITES, it tears, it shakes. [chuckles] It doesn't do whole. [mischievously] Honestly? Jaws lied to you. Verdict: NO.

*Moods: menace → dread → fascination → relief → cheeky → final.*

*Frame: the great white's mouth from below, teeth, the classic. Beside "you". On "a seal", a seal silhouette at the surface, the shark breaching up at it (cut away before contact; no blood). On "Jaws lied to you", a film-reel sprocket frame around the shark, then torn off. NO.*

Sources: about 6 m (20 ft), possibly 6.4 m (21 ft); most recorded fatal unprovoked attacks of any species (Florida Museum / ISAF); bites and tears pinnipeds.

### 5 · The game (2:05–2:20)

> [warmly] Quick one: every animal you're seeing is drawn for a game called InkFish. You start as the smallest fish in the sea, and eat your way up, one bite at a time. [excited] Chapter one is free in your browser, at sixteen-bit dot ink!

*The game's level-one scene: the inkling eats a few minnows and grows. "16bit.ink" in the corner, no URL.*

### 6 · Saltwater crocodile, 20 ft (2:20–2:45)

> [nervous] Now, the one that actually HAS eaten people. The saltwater crocodile: over twenty feet, and more than two THOUSAND pounds. [surprised] It can't chew, so it swallows small prey whole… [chuckles] it even swallows ROCKS! [dramatically] But anything big? It rolls… and tears it apart. Verdict: NO. Not whole.

*Moods: dread → awe → shock → silly → grim → final.*

*Re-hook: a croc's eyes at the waterline, then its jaws snapping open towards the camera. Beside "you" it's three and a half "yous". On "ROCKS", stones rattle into a cut-away of its belly. On "It rolls", the croc spins in a death roll as a dark blur, no prey shown. NO, with "NOT WHOLE" under it.*

Sources: Lolong, 6.17 m (20 ft 3 in), 1,075 kg (2,370 lb), Guinness; swallows small prey whole, can't chew, death-rolls large prey, swallows stones (gastroliths); 2023 Kennedy Bend, Queensland (human remains in a crocodile).

### 7 · Greenland shark, 21 ft (2:45–3:15)

> [curious] Then there's the Greenland shark: up to twenty-one feet, and it swims slower than you walk. [surprised] But scientists have found a whole REINDEER inside one! [chuckles] A piece of polar bear jaw, too. And one choked on a chunk of MOOSE. [softly] It lives in freezing water, far from people… [mischievously] and mostly eats things that were already dead. Verdict: NO.

*Moods: eerie → shock → dark laugh → lonely → sly → final.*

*Dark green-black Arctic water, ice above. The Greenland shark's slow mouth (from the greenland short). A walking silhouette above the ice overtakes it. On "REINDEER", antlers inside a belly cut-away. On "polar bear" and "MOOSE", a small jaw and a moose-hide scrap. NO.*

Sources: about 6.4 m (21 ft); "an entire reindeer and parts of a horse" (Florida Museum); a ~10 cm piece of young polar bear jaw, Svalbard, 2008; the 2013 Newfoundland shark choking on moose hide (RCI); cruising ~0.34 m/s (0.76 mph), slower than walking pace (~3 mph).

### 8 · Whale shark, 40 ft (3:15–3:40)

> [excited] Okay. Let's go BIG. The whale shark: the biggest fish alive, as long as a school bus… [surprised] with a mouth almost FIVE feet wide! [nervous] You'd fit in that mouth easily. [chuckles] Its throat, though? About four INCHES. [warmly] It eats tiny things, and if you got in, it'd just spit you out. Verdict: NO.

*Moods: hype → awe → nervous → silly → gentle → final.*

*Re-hook 2, the first giant: a zoom out as the whale shark crosses the screen, mouth open, a school bus outline behind it. "You" stand inside the mouth's outline with room to spare. On "four INCHES", the throat ring shrinks to a coin-sized hole; the shoulder bar can't even reach it. NO.*

Sources: biggest living fish, commonly ~12 m (40 ft); mouth up to ~1.5 m (5 ft) wide; oesophagus about 10 cm (4 in) wide (E. W. Gudger); spits out unwanted objects (Phil Motta).

### 9 · Humpback whale, 50 ft (3:40–4:10)

> [dramatically] Which brings us back to the humpback. [nervous] In 2021, off Cape Cod, a lobster diver named Michael Packard felt a bump… and everything went DARK. [surprised] He was inside its mouth for thirty seconds! [softly] He thought: this is it. [excited] Then the whale came up… and SPAT him out! [curious] A humpback's throat stretches to about fifteen inches. Your shoulders: sixteen. [chuckles] Close. But no.

*Moods: return → dread → shock → despair → joy → curious → laugh.*

*Closes loop 1. Green Cape Cod water, a diver's silhouette; a vast shadow; everything goes black (only the narration and a heartbeat). On "SPAT him out", light floods back and the diver tumbles out at the surface. The shoulder test: the bar presses into a 15-inch ring and stops, an inch short. NO.*

Sources: 11 June 2021, off Provincetown, MA, ~14 m (45 ft) deep; "felt this huge bump, and everything went dark"; "30 to 40 seconds" in the closed mouth, then spat out at the surface ("this is it", NPR/KAXE, Al Jazeera); throat "about the size of a human fist", stretching to about 15 in (38 cm) (National Geographic).

### 10 · Sperm whale, 60 ft (4:10–4:55)

> [dramatically] So who's left? [nervous] The sperm whale. Up to sixty feet of whale, hunting giant squid in the dark… [surprised] and it can swallow them WHOLE! [curious] Out of about ninety kinds of whale, it's the only one with a throat big enough to swallow a person. [excited] A big male really COULD! [sarcastic] And yes, there's a famous story: 1891, a sailor named James Bartley, swallowed by a sperm whale and found alive inside it. [chuckles] It's fake. His ship wasn't even a whaler. [softly] Because here's the twist… there's no record of ANY whale ever swallowing a person. [warmly] The one animal that could just isn't interested. [mischievously] It wants squid. You're not squid.

*Moods: suspense → dread → shock → wonder → hype → sceptical → laugh → hush → warm → cheeky.*

*Closes loop 2. The deep: black water, the sperm whale's long lower jaw sweeping into the light around a giant squid (the kraken short's art). Beside "you", it's ten "yous" long. The shoulder test: the bar slips straight through the ring; a yellow COULD stamp. On "1891", a sepia newspaper clipping "SWALLOWED BY A WHALE!"; on "fake", a red HOAX stamp. On "no record", the stamp card: eight mouths, seven NOs and one COULD, with "…but never has" under it. On "You're not squid", "you" shrug; the whale swims off after a squid.*

Sources: males up to ~16–18 m (52–59 ft); swallows giant squid (often whole; beaks and squid in stomachs); the only one of ~90 whale species whose throat could technically swallow a human (National Geographic; Joy Reidenberg: a very big adult bull); the 1891 *Star of the East* / James Bartley story is a hoax (Edward B. Davis; exposed in 1907); no whale has ever been documented eating a person (Science News, 2026-09-10).
