InkHeroes Art Directions

Yes, pixel art can keep one pixel density everywhere. Below is the same battle drawn on a locked grid at 16-bit, 32-bit and 64-bit density, and in an ink palette, next to the current painted art. New: the same battle made with PixelLab, a pixel-art generator, at all three densities (option E).

How the density stays locked

  • One native canvas. The whole game renders at a fixed low resolution (16-bit: 320×180, 32-bit: 640×360, 64-bit: 1280×720).
  • Whole-number upscaling only. That canvas is enlarged ×2, ×3, ×4… to fill the screen, never ×2.5. Leftover space becomes a letterbox. Phaser: pixelArt: true, roundPixels: true, integer zoom.
  • Every sprite authored on that grid. No sprite is scaled, rotated or drawn at a sub-pixel position. Bigger creatures get more pixels, not bigger pixels.
  • Every asset made at its final size. Whether drawn in code (option A) or generated by PixelLab (option E), each sprite is produced at the density it is shown at, with one light direction and outline rule, so 15+ creatures still look like one set.

Grid and palette check

  • Grid: passes. All 12 sprites and backgrounds are true 1:1 pixels (no hidden 2×2 blocks), with hard 1-bit edges (no soft alpha), placed at whole-pixel offsets.
  • Palette: not shared. Each generation brings its own 41–84 colours, so a scene has 220–340. They still look like one set because the prompt fixes the style and light. Forcing one 64-colour palette afterwards ruined the sprites (the griffin turned olive). A real shared palette has to be set at generation time, through PixelLab's palette option on its Pro tools.
  • Consistency took re-rolls. 4 of 16 images were redone. The first background drew a fake game HUD, the first 16-bit knight lost its blue, the first 16-bit wolf was muddy, and the first 64-bit background turned into a dark forest. The fast model takes no style reference, so the style holds only through the prompt.

Other directions

Four options that fit a Heroes-style game

A

Pixel art (16, 32 or 64-bit)

The look of the 90s strategy games this genre came from. Units stay readable, and every creature shares one grid, palette and light.

Fit
Strongest for the genre and the 16bit.ink name
Cost
Redraw all art on the grid; more detail per step up
B

Ink pixel art

Option A's sprites in sepia, ink and two washes. Looks like an engraved field manual.

Fit
Matches the brand; weaker readability in battle
Cost
Same as A; a palette swap of it
C

Woodcut storybook

Bold black contours, flat colour fills and carved hatching, like an illustrated chronicle. No washes or soft gradients.

Fit
Keeps the ink identity, reads like a board game
Cost
Lowest: same vector code, simpler fills
D

Tabletop miniatures

Units are painted figures on round bases, the map is a printed game board with tokens, and battles happen on a felt hex mat.

Fit
Natural for turn-based play; most distinct look
Cost
High: needs a strong lighting model
E

Generated pixel art (PixelLab)

Option A's locked grid, but every sprite and background comes from PixelLab instead of code. Looks like a finished 90s-style strategy game.

Fit
Strongest look so far; style holds through the prompt only
Cost
~1 generation per still; animation and a shared palette need a paid plan
Recommendation

Go with E: PixelLab at 32-bit (640×360)

The generated sprites fix what was wrong with option A: they read as real game art. 32-bit is still the right density. A full 640×360 background is a single generation, units have room for faces and armour, and the screen scales by a whole number to 1080p, 1440p and 4K. 16-bit looks great too and would be cheaper to animate, so keep it as the fallback. 64-bit has the richest detail, but a 1280×720 background needs four or more stitched tiles and 1080p screens need a ×1.5 scale.

Before redrawing the game, test two open questions on the battle screen: animation (idle, attack and death for three units) and a shared palette set at generation time. Both need a paid PixelLab plan; the trial runs one job at a time and has 20 generations left.
